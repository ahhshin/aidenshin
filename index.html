About me

Hi, I’m Aiden. I’ve always been deeply curious about the natural world around me and how everything works. I spent my childhood immersed in several obsessions, most notably backcountry canoe camping and Scrabble, where I competed in the international Scrabble championship twice (yes, that’s a thing).

While studying economics and business at Western University, I became deeply interested in learning about AI and building new things with it. This really kicked off during my first-year internship, where I began experimenting with AI to develop a statistical market research engine.

Now, AI in 2023 was substantially more limited than it is today, but working with it to build real things back then kicked off how I would spend the next couple of years.

Since then, I’ve applied myself to a number of projects across the AI space, trying to build things of real value in the real world and building smaller, fun projects to supplement my learning about new areas.

Today, I spend my days working in AI strategy and tech consulting, and my nights and weekends exploring new ways that AI can drive real change in the world.

Learning by building

Where it started

During my summer internship in 2023, I designed and built an interactive 3D map to support business development. The question behind it was where a business should expand next.

I used web scraping and other data-collection techniques to bring together thousands of request-for-proposal (RFP) records and satellite data. Then I built statistical models to explore potential projects, competition in different areas and the revenue an expansion might support.

The goal was to make a complicated location decision easier to investigate. For me, it was also an introduction to using AI to work through unfamiliar problems. I kept finding other things I wanted to try building.

Small projects with real questions

Some projects were practical. At home, I built a web tool to control the sound systems around the house, giving me one place to manage the setup.

Others were a way to learn. I taught myself reinforcement learning and neural networks by training small models on my laptop to play Block Blast. The game gave me something tangible to experiment with as I tried to understand how a model learns.

Building became part of how I learned. Sometimes I ended up with a tool I could use, and sometimes with a better understanding of the technology.

Learning to coordinate agents

Along the way, I began experimenting with n8n and Gumloop. Research I worked on with Ivey Business School gave me another problem to explore: automating parts of bug-bounty research and tracking.

I built research agents around that work and started learning how to coordinate several agents on the same task. That meant figuring out how to divide the work, pass information between steps and bring the results together.

I began thinking more about the system around a model, including the tools it could use, the context it needed and how its work should be checked.

Projects and current work

Making company knowledge more useful

I’ve also designed retrieval-augmented generation systems for corporate settings, with the aim of making a company’s own knowledge and intellectual property more accessible to its employees.

Case Study Retriever is a small public illustration of that idea. It organizes case studies into searchable records with page-level evidence, so someone can find a useful example and check the claim behind it. The demo currently uses offline metadata search and includes a harness for future model integration.

Explore Case Study Retriever (GitHub)

Veridrome

Veridrome was an experiment in using computer-use and vision models for QA automation. I built a prototype around agents interacting with software interfaces and pitched the idea to venture capital investors.

It was also part of my path as a founder: taking something I was building and putting a company idea around it. I eventually changed direction and pivoted from Veridrome to pursue NodeSearch.

NodeSearch at Grenadir Labs

I’m now developing NodeSearch with my co-founder, Alp Unsal, at Grenadir Labs. We’re exploring how AI can help researchers find useful ideas and methods beyond their usual field of view.

The question is simple: could an approach from another lab or discipline help with the problem you’re working on? NodeSearch is being built to surface those possibilities and show enough source evidence for a researcher to judge them.

Our early prototype brings publications, extracted ideas and evidence into a research workspace. A researcher can begin with a paper or project brief, describe a goal and gap, then inspect candidate contributions. The workspace supports evidence review and discussion briefs; a repeatable live discovery pipeline is the next step.

We’re still early, and we need to evaluate the product’s scientific usefulness with researchers. The longer-term direction is proactive discovery: helping useful methods and ideas reach someone who might be able to use them.
